I recently had a paper published in the Wilson Journal of Ornithology: [Neotype designation for Red-winged Blackbird (*Agelaius phoeniceus*)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15594491.2026.2737543).  
  
In short: Some dudes in the early 1900s tried to say Linnaeus described the bird based on a single description from South Carolina, but he had actually included many descriptions from across North and Central America. The S.C. bird also may have included multiple subspecies, so it was unclear what species the Red-winged Blackbird really is, as there was no type specimen. So I chose one bird - just one - to be the new archetypical Red-winged Blackbird going forward.  
  
I wrote a public-facing blog about this for the journal [here](https://wilsonsociety.org/2026/11/19/fourteen-ways-of-looking-at-a-red-winged-blackbird/).
  
This is my first time setting a neotype, an important part of taxonomic nomenclature. Strangely, no code was written as part of this paper. But if you view taxonomy as a global commons, this really isn't all that different from my open source or linguistics work.

There were a couple of interesting moments in this paper, for me. One was realizing that Eleazer Albin has gotten all of the credit for his work, although his daughter is listed on the title page of his work as the illustrator of many of the drawings. There's no reason not to cite both of them when referring to his work. So I did that. 

Another was that the type locality that has been assumed was in Charleston, S.C. – very close to where I was born. I was able to choose a neotype from the same location, which means it is one of the few birds that has been named in the same place where I was first named, by some definitions.
