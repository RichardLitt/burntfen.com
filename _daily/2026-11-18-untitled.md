A teaching day, today. I didn't wake up as early as I normally would, but I was prepared enough for class. One of my students laughed when I imitated a zombie process in C, walking slowly across the stage with my arms out. The bell rang on the last main slide. 

Talking to Julia afterwards, I mentioned that I don't work very hard. She laughed: "You had one bad day teaching, and now you wake up at 3:00 every morning." I conceded her point. Whatever I learned that first day of finishing early, I appear to have learned how not to be in that situation again. I haven't felt the need to flee everyone after teaching; when class goes well, I feel fine now. 

Besides that, I had another achievement today. From the age of 8 to 14 or so, I attended summer camp in the Adirondacks. There were different instructional areas we attended most days: Swimming, Sailing, Canoeing; Fishing, Handcraft, Archery, Riflery; Campcraft, Wildlife, Survival, Tripping. We had to earn a Basic badge in Swimming before we could go to Canoeing or Sailing; I learned the breast stroke, the side, and the crawl, although I was never the best at any of them. I remember the shame of not getting any badges at all my first year, and having to go to Swimming every day, no matter the weather. 

Later, after I had gotten a Basic badge in every instructional area, I focused on Wildlife. After I got my Masters – which took some effort, including learning bird songs and teaching for ten hours – I decided that that was the only instructional area I really cared about. So I figured I would get my Basic and Advanced in everything else. Very few campers took this approach, but  even then, I was happier being a competent generalist. After getting my advanced in everything (even Riflery), I started on my Masters. 

One of the tasks was swimming a mile across the lake and back. I remember the afternoon still, swimming with a lazy backstroke for what seemed like hours, accompanied by a few other boys and someone in a rowboat. Swimming long distances isn't that hard if you just float with a direction in mind. Still, it seemed like an achievement. I'd swum a mile! 

I didn't replicate that feat until today, two decades later. For the first time here, I went to the school 50m pool, and chose a lane in the early afternoon, after class. I swam lazily for 8 minutes, and then hard for 4, easy 4, hard 4, easy 4, hard 4, and then easy four. For the hard laps, I did a front crawl, breathing under my right elbow every stroke. I don't feel like my form is very good, but whatever it was, it was hard.

By the time my planned workout was done, given some extra clock fiddling in between some laps, it had been 30 minutes, and I'd swum 1300m. I wanted to keep going to 1600. So I did. The knowledge that I wasn't stopping until then made it easier, and the last 300m were more rhythmic and calmer than the first 1000. I've felt this in running before, too. The first three miles are the hardest; after 4, you stop caring, and just keep going. Breath, stroke, breath, stroke. 

A lap before the end, I paused for a few seconds to catch my breath. Next to me, there were two teenagers in their own lanes. One of them reached a hand over to me, offering a fist bump. He might have said something which I didn't hear over my underwater headphones and ear plugs. I smiled, laughed, and dove back in.
